You have built the case in your own working life. You deliver strong results, you have made yourself efficient, and you would like some of that efficiency back as time, perhaps a shorter week or a compressed one. The problem is that you cannot simply announce it. You have to ask, and you have to ask someone who holds more power than you do. This is a negotiation, and there is a well-tested way to do it well.

## What framework should you use?

The classic guide is the negotiation method set out in Getting to Yes by Roger Fisher and William Ury: principled negotiation.[^1] Its central instruction is to focus on interests, not positions. A position is what you say you want ("I want Fridays off"). An interest is why you want it, and, crucially, what the other side actually cares about underneath their stance. Position against position produces a standoff. Interest against interest produces room to deal.

So before you ask, map the interests. Yours might be sustainable energy, focus, a life outside work. Your employer's are almost certainly results, reliability, and no drop in the quality or availability of your output. The deal you are looking for is one that serves both: you get time, they get the same or better results. If you can frame your request as serving their interests and not just yours, you have stopped asking for a favour and started proposing a mutually good arrangement.

## How should you actually open?

Lead with your reasoning before your conclusion. A practical maxim from the same book: present your interests and your logic first, so the other party can follow the path to your request rather than having a demand dropped on them.[^2] If you open with "I want a four-day week," you invite an immediate no. If you open with "I have been thinking about how to sustain my best work and keep my results strong, and I have an idea I would like to test," you bring them along the reasoning before they reach the ask.

Then propose it as a trial, not a permanent change. Specify a defined period and objective measures of success agreed in advance, so the decision in front of your boss is small and reversible rather than large and risky. You are not asking them to bet the farm. You are asking them to run a low-cost experiment with a clear way to call it off. That is a far easier yes.

## What gives you leverage?

Your alternatives. The concept of the BATNA, your best alternative to a negotiated agreement, captures the truth that your negotiating power comes chiefly from the quality of your options.[^3] If your results are strong and you could credibly work elsewhere on better terms, your request carries weight, because the cost of refusing you is real. If your work is replaceable and you have nowhere to go, you are negotiating from weakness, and no technique fully fixes that. This is why career capital, building rare and valuable skills, is the foundation under any successful negotiation for autonomy. You earn the right to ask by being worth keeping.

## What if the answer is no?

Sometimes it will be, and the honest reckoning is that an individual asking a more powerful party for time can lose, however well they argue. If so, your options narrow to building more leverage, finding an employer whose culture already fits, or reclaiming time more quietly through the methods you do control. None of these is a perfect answer, because the deepest fix to this problem is not individual at all. It is collective and structural, the four-day-week policies and results-based norms that remove the need to negotiate alone. Until those arrive, principled negotiation is the best tool an individual has: it will not win every time, but it turns a hopeful request into a reasoned proposal, which is the difference between being dismissed and being taken seriously.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you negotiate a shorter working week?**

Use principled negotiation: focus on interests rather than positions, lead with your reasoning before your request, propose it as a trial with clear success metrics, and strengthen your case with a strong alternative so you are not negotiating from weakness.

**What is the best way to ask your boss for fewer hours?**

Frame it around shared interests, your results and reliability, rather than your personal preference. Present your reasoning first so they can follow the logic, and offer a low-risk trial with objective measures of success.

**What gives you leverage to ask for a shorter week?**

Your alternatives and your value. The stronger your results and the better your options elsewhere, your BATNA, the more credibly you can ask, because your negotiating power comes chiefly from the quality of your alternatives.
