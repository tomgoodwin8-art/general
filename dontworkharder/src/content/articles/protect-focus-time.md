You did the work. You killed the report nobody read, delegated the shallow tasks to a well-briefed machine, and one Tuesday an afternoon opened up, genuinely free. Fast forward two weeks. You are exactly as busy as before, and you could not tell anyone where the afternoon went. It simply filled, quietly and from all sides, until the ground was level again.

That vanishing afternoon is the most predictable event in the whole method. Reclaimed time is a vacuum, and a vacuum in your working life does not stay empty. Defending it is the move the entire method turns on.

## Why won't willpower protect it?

Because willpower is a finite resource that depletes under stress, fatigue, and pressure, which means it runs out at precisely the moments the forces are pressing hardest.[^1] Relying on resolve to defend your time is like relying on a sandbag wall that dissolves in rain. It is there in the calm and gone in the storm, and the storm is when you needed it. This is why the four-day-week trials worked: they did not ask people to try harder, they re-engineered the structure of the week, where the defence did not depend on anyone's mood.

## What is the first defence?

Take the reclaimed time and put it on the calendar as a fixed, named, defended block, then treat it the way you would treat surgery: not an intention, but an appointment that cannot be moved. Cal Newport's framing is the one to hold onto: schedule deep-work blocks like meetings and make them non-negotiable, because even ninety minutes of genuinely protected time can produce more of value than eight hours of fragmented, notification-riddled work.[^2]

## What is the test of a real boundary?

This decides everything: a block of time that other people can freely schedule over is not a boundary. It is a suggestion.[^3] If your "focus time" yields the instant someone drops a meeting on it, you do not have a focus block, you have a polite fiction. The wall only counts if it holds when pushed.

## What are the practical tools?

They are all commitment devices wearing work clothes. A standing no-meeting day, or no-meeting mornings, that everyone knows about, the best-known being the no-meeting Wednesday companies like Asana adopted, though the principle scales down to one person.[^4] A hard stop at the end of the day, marked by a small shutdown ritual so the work stops following you home. Reactive work, email and chat, herded into two or three named windows instead of bleeding across every hour. Meetings defaulted to twenty-five minutes rather than thirty. Teams that protect focus this way report real gains, though the crispest numbers come from internal company studies and should be read as directional.[^5]

## How do you avoid overdoing it?

Do not build walls so rigid they shatter on contact with reality. A focus block with zero flexibility collides with a genuine emergency, breaks, and then gets abandoned in all-or-nothing disappointment. Consistency beats perfection, and a shortened or shifted block is worth infinitely more than a skipped one.[^6] Some availability is genuinely part of the job. The goal is a wall with a gate you control, not a wall with no door. Build the structure once, while you are calm, and it will defend the time later, when you are not.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do you protect focus time so it actually holds?**

Put it on the calendar as a fixed, named, defended block and treat it like an appointment that cannot be moved, not an intention. The test is simple: a block others can freely schedule over is not a boundary, it is a suggestion.

**Why does focus time keep getting eaten?**

Because reclaimed time is a vacuum, and Parkinson's Law, rising expectations, and other people's urgency rush to fill it. Willpower fails under pressure, so the defence has to be structural, built into how the week is arranged.

**What are practical ways to defend focus time?**

No-meeting days or mornings, a hard stop with a shutdown ritual, reactive work batched into two or three named windows, and shorter default meeting lengths. Keep a gate you control rather than a wall with no door.

[^2]: Cal Newport, *Deep Work* (2016): schedule protected deep-work blocks as non-negotiable; modest amounts of truly focused time outperform much longer fragmented stretches.

[^3]: A practitioner maxim consistent with the deep-work literature: a focus block others can freely schedule over does not function as a boundary.
