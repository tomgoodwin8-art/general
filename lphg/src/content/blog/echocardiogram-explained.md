## What an echocardiogram is

An echocardiogram, often shortened to an "echo", is an **ultrasound scan of the
heart**. A small probe placed on the chest sends high-frequency sound waves
through the body; the echoes that bounce back are turned into a moving picture of
the heart working in real time. The standard version is a **transthoracic
echocardiogram**, meaning the probe sits on the outside of the chest wall.

It uses no radiation and no needles. That makes it a safe, repeatable way to look
at the structure and function of the heart.

## What an echocardiogram shows

The scan captures several things at once. It shows the size and thickness of the
four heart chambers, how well the muscle is pumping, and whether the four valves
open and close properly. Using Doppler, it also shows the direction and speed of
blood flow, which reveals leaking or narrowed valves.

One of the most important measurements is the **ejection fraction** — the
proportion of blood the main pumping chamber pushes out with each beat. It is a
key marker in diagnosing and monitoring heart failure. The scan can also detect
fluid around the heart, congenital holes, and clots.

## What an echocardiogram cannot show

An echo is not a complete cardiac assessment on its own. It does not directly
show the coronary arteries, so it cannot diagnose narrowed arteries or predict a
heart attack in the way a CT coronary angiogram can. It does not record the
heart's electrical rhythm over time — that is the job of an ECG or a Holter
monitor.

Image quality can also be limited in some people by body shape or lung tissue. In
those cases a cardiologist may recommend a different or additional test.

## Why an echocardiogram may be requested

A doctor may suggest an echo if you have breathlessness, ankle swelling, chest
discomfort, palpitations or a heart murmur heard during examination. It is also
used to monitor known conditions such as valve disease or high blood pressure, and
to check heart function before certain treatments.

## How to prepare

No special preparation is needed. Eat, drink and take your usual medicines as
normal. Wear clothing that gives easy access to the chest, as you will change into
a gown and lie on your left side during the scan. The room is dimmed so the
sonographer can see the screen clearly.

## Understanding your report

Your report will describe each chamber and valve, give the ejection fraction as a
percentage, and note any abnormal flow. Terms like "mild mitral regurgitation" or
"preserved systolic function" are common and often describe minor or expected
findings. A reporting cardiologist interprets the images in the context of your
symptoms, so the numbers are always read alongside how you feel.
