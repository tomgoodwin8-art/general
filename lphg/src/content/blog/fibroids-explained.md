## What fibroids are

Fibroids, known medically as **uterine leiomyomas**, are non-cancerous growths made
of muscle and fibrous tissue that develop in or around the uterus. They are very
common, affecting a large proportion of women at some point, most often between the
ages of 30 and 50. They range from the size of a pea to, occasionally, a melon.

Fibroids are grouped by where they sit: **submucosal** fibroids bulge into the
cavity of the uterus, **intramural** fibroids grow within the muscle wall, and
**subserosal** fibroids extend outwards from the surface. Position matters, because
it strongly influences which symptoms, if any, a fibroid causes.

## What a scan shows

A **pelvic ultrasound** is the standard way to assess fibroids. Using sound waves
and no radiation, it shows the number, size and position of fibroids, and whether
they are distorting the cavity of the uterus. It can be performed through the
abdomen, through the vagina for a closer view, or both.

The scan measures each significant fibroid so that growth can be tracked over time,
and it checks the ovaries and the lining of the womb at the same time. For most
women this is all that is needed to explain symptoms and guide a plan.

## What a scan cannot show

Ultrasound is excellent at detecting and measuring fibroids, but it has limits. It
cannot tell you with certainty whether a growth is a fibroid or, very rarely,
something more concerning, though the appearance is usually reassuring. Very large
or numerous fibroids can be hard to map fully by ultrasound alone.

In those situations an MRI may be added, as it gives a more detailed map of size,
position and blood supply, which is particularly useful when planning surgery or
procedures such as embolisation.

## Why a scan may be requested

A fibroid scan is often arranged for heavy or prolonged periods, pelvic pressure or
swelling, frequent urination, or difficulty conceiving. It is also used to monitor
known fibroids, to check whether they are growing, and to clarify a mass felt
during an examination.

## How to prepare

Preparation depends on the type of scan. For an abdominal ultrasound you are usually
asked to drink water beforehand and arrive with a comfortably full bladder, which
pushes the bowel aside and improves the view. A transvaginal scan is done with an
empty bladder. Wearing a two-piece outfit makes the appointment easier. We confirm
the exact instructions when you book.

## Understanding your report

Your report will list the number of fibroids, give the dimensions of the larger
ones in millimetres or centimetres, and describe their type and location. It will
also comment on the lining of the womb and the ovaries. Because symptoms depend on
position as much as size, a small submucosal fibroid can matter more than a large
subserosal one, so the report is best read alongside your symptoms.
