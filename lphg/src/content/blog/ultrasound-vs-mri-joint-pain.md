    a: For deep or complex structures, MRI usually gives more complete information. For superficial tendons and soft tissues, ultrasound can be just as accurate and has the advantage of showing movement in real time. The right test depends on the suspected diagnosis.

  - q: Does ultrasound use radiation?
    a: No. Ultrasound uses sound waves and involves no radiation, which is why it can be repeated freely and used in pregnancy. MRI also uses no radiation; it uses a strong magnetic field and radio waves. CT, by contrast, does use X-ray radiation.
  - q: Can I have an ultrasound-guided injection at the same time?
    a: Often yes. One advantage of ultrasound is that it can guide a therapeutic injection precisely to the affected tendon or joint during the same visit, if that is clinically appropriate and you consent to it.

## What these scans are

When a joint hurts, imaging helps show why. Three tools are used most often.
**Ultrasound** uses sound waves to build a live picture of soft tissues near the
surface. **MRI**, or magnetic resonance imaging, uses a strong magnetic field and
radio waves to produce detailed cross-sectional images of both soft tissue and
bone. **CT**, or computed tomography, uses X-rays to give fast, detailed pictures
of bone.

Each works differently, and each has jobs it does well. Choosing the right one
depends far more on the suspected problem than on which is "best".

## What each scan shows

Ultrasound is excellent for tendons, muscles, bursae and soft-tissue swelling, and
it is the only one of the three that shows the joint moving in real time. That
dynamic view is valuable for problems such as tendon impingement or a tendon that
catches. It also guides injections precisely.

MRI shows the widest range of structures: cartilage, deep ligaments, the menisci of
the knee, and changes within the bone marrow that no other scan reveals. It is the
usual choice for the inside of large joints and for unexplained deep pain. CT is
the strongest for detailed bone anatomy, such as complex fractures.

| Feature | Ultrasound | MRI | CT |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Best for | Tendons, muscles, bursae, soft-tissue swelling | Cartilage, ligaments, menisci, bone marrow, deep joints | Detailed bone, complex fractures |
| Real-time / dynamic imaging | Yes | No | No |
| Radiation | None | None | X-ray radiation |
| Guides injections | Yes, during the scan | Not routinely | Occasionally |
| Typical scan time | 20 to 30 minutes | 20 to 45 minutes | 5 to 15 minutes |
| Enclosed space | No | Yes, inside the scanner | Partly |
| Metal implants a concern | No | Yes, requires screening | Usually no |
| Relative cost | Lower | Higher | Moderate |

## What each scan cannot show

Ultrasound cannot see through bone, so it cannot assess cartilage inside a joint,
the menisci, or bone marrow. Deep structures within the hip or the centre of the
knee are largely beyond it.

MRI, despite its detail, is slower, more expensive, and unsuitable for some people
with certain implants or severe claustrophobia. It also does not show tissue in
motion. CT gives limited soft-tissue detail and, unlike the other two, uses
ionising radiation, so it is reserved for questions about bone.

## Why a particular scan may be requested

A clinician chooses the scan to match the question. Suspected tendon or muscle
problems, or a lump near the surface, point towards ultrasound. Suspected cartilage
damage, a meniscal tear, ligament rupture or deep unexplained pain point towards
MRI. A complex bony injury points towards CT. Often ultrasound is a sensible,
lower-cost first step that answers the question without an MRI.

## How to prepare

An MSK ultrasound needs no special preparation; simply wear clothing that allows
access to the affected area. For an MRI you complete a safety questionnaire and
remove all metal, as the magnet is always on; tell the team about any implants,
a pacemaker or possible metal fragments. For CT, follow the specific instructions
you are given, which occasionally involve contrast dye.

## Understanding your report

Your report describes the structures examined and any abnormality found, such as
"tendinopathy", "a partial tear" or "an effusion". Findings are always weighed
against your symptoms, because scans frequently show age-related changes that cause
no pain. A reporting radiologist, and where relevant an orthopaedic clinician,
interprets the images in that context rather than treating the picture alone.

## Costs

The honest market picture: in London a single-region private MRI typically costs
from around £300 to £700, and more when a consultant consultation is bundled in or
several areas are scanned. CT sits in a broadly similar range. An MSK ultrasound is
generally less expensive and, for many soft-tissue problems, answers the question
without needing an MRI at all.
